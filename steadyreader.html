How it works

After a stroke, reading often breaks down not because words stop making sense, but because the eyes can't travel along a line reliably. A loss of vision on one side (hemianopia) or a tendency to ignore one side (neglect) affects reading differently depending on which side it is.

Which side matters

  • Right side affected: the text ahead of the eyes is missing, so moving along a line is slow. Modes that bring the words to a fixed point (One word, Phrases, Scrolling) remove that step.
  • Left side affected: reading along the line is easier, but finding the start of the next line is hard. Guided lines, with its colored bar at the start of every line and the current line lit up, targets this.

The modes

One word shows words one at a time in the same place. Phrases shows short groups. Guided lines keeps normal text but narrows the column, lights the current line and marks every line start. Word column stacks words top to bottom so the eyes move down, not across. Rotated turns the text 90° so lines run vertically. Scrolling moves text right to left past a fixed mark.

What the research says

Scrolling text is the basis of Read-Right, a free web therapy from UCL; in its first study, people with right-sided hemianopia read static text faster after practice, with more gain the more hours they put in (Ong et al., 2012). A randomized trial of reading text rotated to vertical improved speed mainly for right-sided field loss, while horizontal training helped left-sided loss more (2021 RCT). A 2024 study combined floating text, single-word presentation and a moving window (Kerkhoff & Kraft, 2024). The overall evidence base is still small, so treat these modes as things to try and compare, not a prescription.

For the reader

  • Start / Pause: the big gold button, or the space bar. The highlight moves along each line for you.
  • Lost your place? Look for the red bar on the left. The line it marks is the one to read.
  • Stuck on a word? Tap it and it will be read out loud.
  • Too fast or too slow? Use Slower and Faster. Text too small? Use A+ at the top.
  • Want to listen too? Turn on “Read to me” and it reads along with the highlight.
  • Opening an email attachment: save the attachment first, then choose “Open a document”. For a paper letter, use “Photo of a page”.

Getting started

  • Start slow (around 80–120 words per minute) and raise the speed only when reading feels easy.
  • Try each mode for a few minutes. Keep the one that feels least tiring.
  • Short daily sessions add up. The ring at the top tracks time against a daily goal.
  • Show the settings to his occupational therapist or neuro-optometrist: they can say which side is affected and whether it's field loss or neglect.

Keyboard

Space start/pause · → next · ← back · ↑/↓ faster/slower · R read aloud · H hear it · 1–6 switch mode

Privacy

Files are processed in this browser and saved only on this device. Reading photos of pages (OCR) downloads a text-recognition engine the first time it's used.

This is a reading aid, not a medical device or a substitute for therapy.
